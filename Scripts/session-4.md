### Slide 2

The student sits at the centre of these intersecting partnerships. Families, school staff and support staff bring different knowledge and different relationships with that young person.

In secondary school there may be six or more subject teachers, a year level coordinator, learning support, wellbeing staff and relief teachers. No single person sees everything. Support staff may notice patterns across the day that one teacher cannot see, and their knowledge deserves to be asked for and recognised.

The family may know how the student feels after school, what Sunday evening is like, or what helped in an earlier setting. The student knows things an outside observer cannot tell us: what something feels like, what helps and what is hard.

What should these partnerships make possible for the student? Hold access, participation and their own goals in view. Ask who is missing from the conversation and how the student's communication can be included. Page nineteen of your guide brings these key ideas together.

### Slide 3

Power shapes consultation even when everyone intends to help. Think privately about a meeting you've been part of. Who chose the time, place and agenda? Who understood the language being used? Who could make a decision?

The slide names several influences: socio-economic circumstances, historical and cultural experiences of education, language, understanding of school systems and curriculum, and experiences of school itself. In many meetings, the school sets the arrangements and several staff are present. A family may arrive with a long history of meetings, including experiences in which they didn't feel heard.

Online and phone meetings can also create barriers through timing, technology or how people take turns. Partnership can be difficult for more than one person. We can acknowledge that and still examine what the school can change.

Whose knowledge is heard? Is the student included in a way that works for them? Those questions help us examine the process without labelling a family as difficult or disengaged.

### Slide 6

Useful consultation has a clear shared purpose. It uses open questions, more than one way to contribute and respect for student and family knowledge. We record what we've heard accurately and agree an action, who is responsible and how we'll follow up. The checklist is on page twenty-one.

Consider the difference between asking, Any concerns? and, What does a good morning look like? The second question invites detail that may otherwise remain unseen. A useful follow-up tells the student or family what you did with what they shared and checks whether you've understood it correctly.

The slide also reminds us that, under the Disability Standards for Education 2005, schools must consult the student, or an associate such as a parent or carer, about adjustments. Follow your school and system processes alongside current official guidance.

Ask, listen, and follow up. A form or tool can support that conversation, but it can't do the listening for us.

### Slide 7

The two columns offer practical ways to make consultation accessible. With students, prepare the conversation and make communication choices available. Questions, agendas or simple choices can be shared beforehand. Allow processing time and consider a sensory environment that works for them.

A student might contribute through speech, A A C, writing, drawing, a video or a trusted supporter. That person's role is to assist the student's communication and decision-making. Keep the student's own view at the centre. Notice behaviour as possible communication, while checking meaning rather than assuming you know it.

With families, begin with strengths, interests and what works well. Use plain language, explain educational terms and listen without assuming that your school perspective is complete. Families may have different cultural experiences, and some parents may be neurodivergent themselves.

Use specific examples, check shared understanding and agree what happens next. Choose one practice from each column that could improve the conversation you are preparing.

### Slide 8

Adolescents can move from being talked about, to taking part and, over time, leading their own meetings. That requires preparation and support in a form that works for them.

A student might attend part of a meeting, send a written contribution, share a one-page profile, receive questions in advance, or ask a trusted adult to support them. Joining an online meeting with a camera off or contributing through chat can also be valid choices.

Being physically present without preparation doesn't guarantee participation. Choosing writing or deciding not to attend a particular meeting doesn't mean a student has failed at self-advocacy. We need to understand the choice and make workable routes available.

Speaking up for themselves can be useful practice for senior school, work and life after school. A student who spends the day masking may first need a safer setting and time to prepare. Where you can, ask the student directly, using their preferred ways of communicating. Their learning and support are the subject of the decisions.

### Slide 9

This slide touches on distress, and you are welcome to pause or step away.

When capacity runs out, the response may be outward or inward. A meltdown can be an outward response to overwhelm. A shutdown can be inward, quiet and easier to miss. School Can't describes difficulty attending that needs understanding and support, rather than being treated as wilful defiance.

In secondary school, a shutdown might be mistaken for disengagement. Difficulty attending may develop over time, with partial days or growing dread of school. We shouldn't infer any one explanation from a single sign. The question is what the student is experiencing and what support is needed.

Home and school may see different parts of that picture. School sees demands across periods. Families may see what is left at the end of the day. Focus on safety, what came before, what supports recovery and what can change in the environment, task or response. Use the appropriate school support processes and relevant team. We don't need crisis stories in a group chat to learn from this.

### Slide 11

Belonging, capacity and joy help us judge whether a change supports flourishing. Belonging means being recognised, included and able to participate as oneself. Capacity concerns what the day costs, protects and restores. Joy includes interests, connection and authentic expression.

Keep them together as you review the design. Reducing a barrier matters. So does whether the student feels more included, has more available for the rest of the day, or can take part in something they value.

Grades, attendance and a quiet classroom response won't tell us everything. Across different periods and teachers, we need to keep asking about the student's whole experience. These questions connect our lens, learning design and partnership.

### Slide 13

Now use page twenty-three to plan one real conversation you will have within two weeks. Choose a student's family, the student themselves, or a colleague who teaches them. Where you can, make room for the family or student conversation. A colleague handover can also matter, especially when support needs to travel across the timetable.

Start from your bridge notes and define the purpose. Write what you will share, beginning with a strength. Write what you will ask specifically, and what you can offer. Then write your opening sentence, word for word.

Add when, where and how the conversation could happen. Include the process change you identified earlier. How will the person's communication preferences be supported? How will the student's voice be included, even if this is a conversation between staff? What will happen afterwards, and who will follow up?

Pause for about eight minutes to plan. Keep all notes anonymous. You can check an opening sentence with a colleague if you wish, but keep the response unscripted. Leave real space to listen.
