### Slide 8

Our starting point is neurodiversity affirming. People experience and interact with the world differently. Different ways of knowing, doing, being and belonging deserve recognition. Difference does not make someone a lesser version of another person.

That means we include strengths, interests and joy alongside barriers and support needs. Recognising a student's strengths doesn't make a difficult day disappear. Someone can bring deep knowledge, care and creativity and still need substantial support.

Think of the students on your page. What becomes visible when you include what they enjoy and how they connect? How might that change the language you use about them? Instead of a label such as difficult, we can describe what the environment asks, what the student communicates, and what support might help. We can be specific about a difficulty while respecting the person experiencing it.

### Slide 9

The social model gives us an environment-first lens. We look for barriers in places, tasks, routines and expectations, and ask what could change around the student.

Picture a crowded corridor between periods. There may be noise, unpredictable movement, people brushing past, and very little time to reach the next room. Before deciding that the student needs to cope better, we can ask what options exist. Could information about the route be clearer? Is there a less crowded route or a different transition arrangement worth discussing?

Those are possibilities to explore with the student. We can't know from the corridor alone which one would help. Include their communication and experience, try an appropriate change, and review whether participation becomes more workable. Changing an environment won't remove every difficulty. It gives us something practical and important to examine.

### Slide 10

Autism can be understood as difference and dynamic disability. Access and support needs can change with the task, the environment, relationships and the capacity available at that moment.

In secondary school, that may happen within a single day. A student might participate comfortably in one subject, then find the next period much harder. The room, teacher, instructions, pace and previous demands may all be different. Managing something yesterday doesn't tell us what support is needed today.

So we keep our assumptions flexible. We ask what has changed, what the task requires, and what the student can tell us. Strengths and substantial support needs can both belong in the picture. Support should respond to the person and the situation, rather than to a fixed idea of what that student ought to manage.

### Slide 11

Many of us learnt about five senses: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. This workshop extends that picture to eight.

Proprioception is our sense of where the body is in space. It involves information from our muscles and joints. Think about knowing where your feet are as you move through a busy room, or how much pressure to use when carrying equipment.

The vestibular sense concerns balance and movement. Moving on stairs, changing direction in a game, rocking in a chair, or being still after movement can involve this sense. Different students may seek or avoid different kinds of movement.

Interoception is our sense of signals from inside the body. We'll return to it shortly, when we consider hunger, thirst and feelings.

Across a school day, senses work together. A science laboratory, the canteen, a music room and the bus bay offer quite different experiences. Keep the question open: what is this place like for this student, at this time?

### Slide 12

Sensory experience includes pleasure, connection and regulation, as well as discomfort or overload. We need both parts in view.

Notice sensory joy. It might be a sound, a texture, a movement or another experience the student values. Stimming can also be part of pleasure, regulation and authentic expression. Harmless stimming does not need to be discouraged simply because it looks different.

Think about where you see enjoyment in the students you have in mind. If you aren't sure what an action means for someone, that's a reason to ask in a way they can use. Avoid treating difference as disruption by default. What would the student want other people to understand about that experience?

### Slide 13

This slide introduces detail-first thinking, sometimes described as bottom-up processing. The image builds a whole scene from individual details. It offers a way to think about how someone may notice specifics and build the bigger picture from them.

That can help us notice genuine strengths: recognising patterns, remembering detail, understanding systems, finding inconsistencies, or engaging with a subject very deeply. Honesty and directness, logical precision and depth of connection may also be important parts of a student's experience. ### Slide 14

Now consider where attention goes. The torch and floodlight on the slide show two ways attention can spread. A floodlight covers a wider area. A torch concentrates light more deeply in one place.

Monotropism is a framework for understanding attention that channels into fewer things with greater depth. Developed by Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser and Wenn Lawson, it helps us consider deep interests, transitions and the experience of flow. It is a lens for inquiry, with individual differences still in view.

Think of a student absorbed in coding, a design project, writing, or a topic they love. Their attention is invested there. When the bell goes, stopping and moving to another subject can ask a great deal. A warning, a clear place to stop, and support for the transition may make that shift more manageable.

Deep interests can give access to learning, memory and joy. Full absorption can be a valuable experience. Alongside helping students change activities when needed, we can look for opportunities to protect that depth of engagement.

### Slide 15

Executive functioning gives us language for the demands within a task. Planning is working out the steps. Organisation concerns the materials and information needed. Time management includes judging and using the time available. Working memory helps us hold information while we use it. Meta-cognition means thinking about our own thinking.

The other areas on the slide are emotional regulation, flexibility, attention, task initiation and persistence. They include managing feelings, adapting when something changes, directing attention, getting started and continuing with a task.

Consider an assessment handed out in the last period. The subject knowledge is only part of what it asks. A student also needs to understand the instructions, organise materials, decide where to begin, plan work over time, and remember how to submit it.

These terms are lenses for inquiry. They don't tell us why a particular student is struggling. Ask which demand needs support. Written steps, a visible first action or a clearer schedule may be useful possibilities to investigate, depending on the student and the task.

### Slide 16

Let's return to interoception, our awareness and understanding of internal body signals. Those signals can include temperature, hunger, thirst and needing the bathroom. They help us make sense of how we're doing inside.

Alexithymia means difficulty identifying and describing emotions and feelings. A person might find it hard to distinguish excitement from anxiety, or to find words for what they feel. The questions on the slide remind us that an outward sign doesn't always tell us the inner experience. Crying, for example, does not give us a complete explanation of someone's feelings.

In school, we might ask how a student can communicate a need or access a break when a signal is unclear or hard to describe. We shouldn't assume that appearing calm means feeling comfortable, or that a student can always explain a feeling on demand. Keep listening, allow time and stay curious about what we cannot see.

### Slide 3

Understanding and expressing are related, but they don't necessarily look the same. Understanding concerns information coming in and how a person makes sense of it. Expressing concerns messages going out, through any communication method.

Someone may speak fluently and still need support to understand rapid instructions or unfamiliar subject language. Someone else may understand much more than they can readily express in speech. How much someone speaks does not tell us how much they understand.

A A C stands for augmentative and alternative communication. It can include signs, a communication board or book, typing, or a communication device. Some people use it alongside speech and some use it instead of speech. Communication can also change with the setting, demands and how someone is feeling.

Think privately about your room and your subject. How can students receive information, ask a question, make a choice or show you what they know? Our role here is to improve access and work with the student, family and relevant team. Individual assessment questions belong with that team.

### Slide 5

Double empathy invites us to consider misunderstanding in both directions. When people have different experiences and ways of communicating, understanding each other can take work from everyone involved.

In a class discussion, for example, a teacher may expect a quick spoken answer. A student may need more processing time, written information, or another way to respond. If we see only the missing spoken answer, we may miss the mismatch between expectations and access.

Ask whose expectations are shaping the interaction. What could make connection safer or clearer? Those questions apply to group work, corridors, break times, online spaces, and interactions with different teachers and peers. We can examine our own part in the exchange as well as trying to understand the student's perspective. Connection does not have to look one way.

Students connect with people in family, school, community and online settings. Some enjoy one-to-one conversation. Some enjoy being alongside another person quietly. ### Slide 8

The battery on this slide gives us a simple way to talk about energy and capacity. A day can begin with a different amount of charge. Actions use energy, and when the battery runs low, it may show or it may remain hidden.

Some people describe this through spoons. There is a limited supply, different actions use some of them, and low spoons can make further demands harder to manage. Both images are metaphors that can open a conversation. They don't measure a student's internal state.

The right support can also restore some capacity. A valued activity, a workable break, clearer information or a safer connection may make a difference. What helps will vary. As we map the day, include what gives something back as well as what uses energy.

### Slide 8

Adolescents can move from being talked about, to taking part and, over time, leading their own meetings. That requires preparation and support in a form that works for them.

A student might attend part of a meeting, send a written contribution, share a one-page profile, receive questions in advance, or ask a trusted adult to support them. Joining an online meeting with a camera off or contributing through chat can also be valid choices.

Being physically present without preparation doesn't guarantee participation. Choosing writing or deciding not to attend a particular meeting doesn't mean a student has failed at self-advocacy. We need to understand the choice and make workable routes available.

Speaking up for themselves can be useful practice for senior school, work and life after school. A student who spends the day masking may first need a safer setting and time to prepare. Where you can, ask the student directly, using their preferred ways of communicating. Their learning and support are the subject of the decisions.

### Slide 9

This slide touches on distress, and you are welcome to pause or step away.

When capacity runs out, the response may be outward or inward. A meltdown can be an outward response to overwhelm. A shutdown can be inward, quiet and easier to miss. School Can't describes difficulty attending that needs understanding and support, rather than being treated as wilful defiance.

In secondary school, a shutdown might be mistaken for disengagement. Difficulty attending may develop over time, with partial days or growing dread of school. We shouldn't infer any one explanation from a single sign. The question is what the student is experiencing and what support is needed.

Home and school may see different parts of that picture. School sees demands across periods. Families may see what is left at the end of the day. Focus on safety, what came before, what supports recovery and what can change in the environment, task or response. Use the appropriate school support processes and relevant team. We don't need crisis stories in a group chat to learn from this.

### Slide 11

Belonging, capacity and joy help us judge whether a change supports flourishing. Belonging means being recognised, included and able to participate as oneself. Capacity concerns what the day costs, protects and restores. Joy includes interests, connection and authentic expression.

Keep them together as you review the design. Reducing a barrier matters. So does whether the student feels more included, has more available for the rest of the day, or can take part in something they value.

Grades, attendance and a quiet classroom response won't tell us everything. Across different periods and teachers, we need to keep asking about the student's whole experience. These questions connect our lens, learning design and partnership.
