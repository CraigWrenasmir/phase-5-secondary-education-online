### Slide 2

Students come first. Understanding a student's experience gives us a starting point for design. It should never reduce that person to a list of problems.

Today you'll design one change, then check it against the different students you have in mind. Use what you know from their communication, from the day map and from what others have shared appropriately. Keep unknowns as questions.

Separate an observation from an interpretation. A task wasn't started is an observation. The student didn't care is an interpretation that may overlook the demands involved. A useful brief asks what the task required and what we could change. Keep strengths and interests in view as things to build on, alongside costs the design may need to address.

### Slide 6

Start with assessment. Imagine a capable student misses a task. Before settling on motivation as the explanation, ask what starting, planning and managing time required.

Publishing assessment schedules early makes information available while it can still help. Chunking a task with visible milestones shows intermediate steps. An exemplar can help students see the finished thing they are working towards. Literal submission instructions clarify exactly where, how and when to hand it in.

If information is hard to hold in mind, keep it where students can return to it. Written first steps, instructions that stay on a class page and a visible time cue may be useful. Consider homework too. How much of the task concerns subject knowledge, and how much concerns organising and remembering what to do?

Match support to the known demand and check it with the student. Where assessment policy sits beyond your role, note who needs to be involved. The purpose is to help students access the task and show what they know, while retaining its meaningful learning requirements.

### Slide 7

Predictability means having useful information about routine and change. It cannot promise that nothing unexpected will happen.

Across a secondary timetable, a great deal may be spoken once, implied, or held by adults. A student arriving to a relief teacher, a room change and a different task may have several questions at once. What is happening? What is changing? What comes next? What can I do if the plan changes?

Look at your day map. How would a student find that information if they couldn't rely on remembering a spoken announcement? Could they check the class page or timetable system? Would the usual start routine continue with a relief teacher? How could they ask for clarification in a way they can use?

Give information early enough to matter and in a form that makes sense to the student. Clear notice of change can be just as important as clarity about the ordinary routine.

### Slide 8

A useful support needs to travel across the timetable. What works with one teacher can become unavailable if the next teacher doesn't know about it.

One option is a concise profile written with the student, covering strengths, interests, what helps and what costs. It should reflect their voice and be reviewed with them. Share useful guidance with the relevant teachers, including new and relief teachers, while avoiding unnecessary personal detail.

Agree where the information is kept and who updates it. Consider whether familiar visual supports or ways of explaining a task remain available in different rooms. A document somewhere on a shared drive is only useful if the right person can find and use it when needed.

Would the period four teacher know what helps before the lesson begins? If your brief concerns a handover, that question may be a useful test. Keep the student involved in what is written and shared.

### Slide 9

A visual support should clarify something a student needs to find, understand, remember or communicate. Start with that information need.

In secondary school, a support might be written instructions that remain on a class page, a task map, a checklist or an assessment calendar. It should suit the student's age and be available at the point of use. A visual format doesn't automatically make information understandable.

The quick review on page sixteen asks four questions. Can the student understand it? Can they access it when they need it? Does it reflect what is actually happening, including change? Does it give useful information or a way to respond?

If a student uses A A C, consider both access to information and access to their own communication. Keep their system available through classes and transitions. A quiet or non-spoken response still deserves attention.

The source materials also point to a Visual Supports Checklist and a deeper webinar. Their access links remain to be confirmed by the delivery team. For now, the review questions in your Participant Guide give you something concrete to use.

### Slide 10

Universal Design for Learning helps us consider access before a barrier arises. Make important information clear and available. Offer workable ways to participate and respond. Support engagement while keeping meaningful challenge.

A class might have more than one way to encounter information or demonstrate learning, where those choices fit the learning purpose. What you know about individual students can inform that design, and their experience can help you review it. Having a resource present is only the beginning. We still need to find out whether students can use it.

### Slide 12

On page seventeen, My design, work through five steps. State the barrier or unnecessary demand. Describe the change to the environment, task or routine. Explain how students' communication and experience will be included. Decide what you will observe. Set a time to review it.

Something a teacher could try this week is a useful size. Written as well as spoken instructions, a warning before packing up, or showing an exemplar earlier are examples. Your brief should guide the choice.

Consider who else needs to know, especially if your change involves an assessment or handover. It may include subject teachers, learning support, a year level coordinator or relief staff. A change beyond your role can become a question to take to the appropriate team.

### Slide 14

Support extends across a continuum. An inclusive environment reduces barriers through the ordinary way school is organised. Clear routines across classes and an acceptance of different ways to participate are examples.

Universal supports are available broadly. These might include information in spoken and written forms, exemplars and a class page where instructions remain accessible. Targeted support responds where additional assistance is needed, such as pre-teaching subject vocabulary or checking in around assessment milestones.

Individual adjustments respond to a particular student's access and participation needs. They may involve an individually agreed assessment environment, processing time or ways to show learning, depending on the student and context.

These layers can work together. A useful universal design doesn't remove the need for individual support, and a student shouldn't have to wait for every broader change before their needs are addressed. Connect each response with evidence and consultation, then review its usefulness through what happens in practice.

### Slide 15

This slide places environmental design and Universal Design for Learning alongside levels used in the Nationally Consistent Collection of Data on School Students with Disability, or N C C D.

The four N C C D levels shown are quality differentiated teaching practice, supplementary adjustments, substantial adjustments and extensive adjustments. Reasonable adjustments are the changes or supports that help a student access and participate in education. The N C C D is the process for collecting information about adjustments and the evidence behind them.

Keep those ideas distinct. Our immediate question is what support improves access for this student, informed by consultation and review. The appropriate school or system process guides how that work is documented. This brief overview doesn't replace those processes or individual advice from the relevant school team.
