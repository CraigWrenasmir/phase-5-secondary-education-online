### Slide 2

We move between communication methods throughout the day, often without noticing. The online setting makes some of those choices visible: typing, speaking, reacting, or waiting before replying. Speech is one part of communication. In school, we need to recognise the ways a student is already communicating and make sure those ways are available where they need them.

### Slide 3

Understanding and expressing are related, but they don't necessarily look the same. Understanding concerns information coming in and how a person makes sense of it. Expressing concerns messages going out, through any communication method.

Someone may speak fluently and still need support to understand rapid instructions or unfamiliar subject language. Someone else may understand much more than they can readily express in speech. How much someone speaks does not tell us how much they understand.

A A C stands for augmentative and alternative communication. It can include signs, a communication board or book, typing, or a communication device. Some people use it alongside speech and some use it instead of speech. Communication can also change with the setting, demands and how someone is feeling.

Think privately about your room and your subject. How can students receive information, ask a question, make a choice or show you what they know? Our role here is to improve access and work with the student, family and relevant team. Individual assessment questions belong with that team.

### Slide 5

Double empathy invites us to consider misunderstanding in both directions. When people have different experiences and ways of communicating, understanding each other can take work from everyone involved.

In a class discussion, for example, a teacher may expect a quick spoken answer. A student may need more processing time, written information, or another way to respond. If we see only the missing spoken answer, we may miss the mismatch between expectations and access.

Ask whose expectations are shaping the interaction. What could make connection safer or clearer? Those questions apply to group work, corridors, break times, online spaces, and interactions with different teachers and peers. We can examine our own part in the exchange as well as trying to understand the student's perspective. Connection does not have to look one way.

### Slide 6

Students connect with people in family, school, community and online settings. Some enjoy one-to-one conversation. Some enjoy being alongside another person quietly. Shared interests, gaming, clubs, sport or music can offer important ways to connect. Contact may be frequent or occasional and still be valued.

Culture and individual preference also shape how people interact. We shouldn't assume that the connection we can see at school represents a young person's whole social world. For many adolescents, online communities and shared-interest groups are meaningful places of belonging.

Keep privacy in mind as you reflect. We don't need details about a student's online activity, identity or community membership. The useful question is how each student connects, and what makes that connection accessible to them.

### Slide 8

The battery on this slide gives us a simple way to talk about energy and capacity. A day can begin with a different amount of charge. Actions use energy, and when the battery runs low, it may show or it may remain hidden.

Some people describe this through spoons. There is a limited supply, different actions use some of them, and low spoons can make further demands harder to manage. Both images are metaphors that can open a conversation. They don't measure a student's internal state.

The right support can also restore some capacity. A valued activity, a workable break, clearer information or a safer connection may make a difference. What helps will vary. As we map the day, include what gives something back as well as what uses energy.

Six classrooms, six teachers, one student. Take a moment to picture one student's route through the day. How many rooms, adults, corridor trips and changes of expectation does it involve?

### Slide 13

Masking concerns the effort someone may make to hide or manage parts of themselves in order to fit expectations. Looking only at outward behaviour or task completion can leave a great deal unseen.

Keep these questions private. What might a student be working hard to manage or hide? Where can they feel recognised and included? What supports belonging while allowing them to be themselves? What do they want others to understand?

We can't determine masking from a single behaviour, and visible quietness doesn't give us the whole story. Alongside demands, keep strengths, joy and safe connection in view. The next few slides touch on burnout and School Can't. You're welcome to pause, look away or step away and return whenever you need to.

### Slide 14

The pressure to fit in can be especially strong in the secondary years. Masking may increase as belonging with peers matters more. Working out who you are while also hiding parts of yourself can take a great deal of energy.

Burnout and School Can't can build over time. We will return to partnership with families in Session Four, including how home and school may see different parts of a student's capacity. Here, the point is to notice the demands without blame.

Adolescence is also a time of growth. Young people can build self-understanding and practise communicating what they need when school feels safe. A student may need time, preparation and different ways to express a view. Feeling safe to be themselves helps make that possible. We don't need personal stories in a group chat to consider what a school could do to support that safety.

### Slide 18

Before we close, let the room settle. You can look away from the screen for a moment if that helps.

Across these first two sessions, you've been building a fuller picture of the students you have in mind. Sensory experience, ways of thinking and attention, internal signals, communication and connection all form part of that picture. So do strengths, interests and moments of joy.

Managing demands across rooms, teachers and expectations takes resources. Sometimes there is also the effort of appearing fine when the inner experience is harder than it looks. A young person who knows who they are, and feels valued for it, has more available for other parts of life.

The research discussed in the guide connects a positive Autistic identity with wellbeing. In our everyday work, that invites a practical question: where can this student be recognised and valued as themselves? Real choice matters, including how they communicate, where they sit and when they step back. Connection with other Autistic people can also be important.

Support the students you teach to know themselves, and to know that who they are is worth knowing.

Thank you for joining this session. Keep your map and your Keep, Reduce, Explore notes. Session Three turns them into a practical change that you can try, review and refine.
